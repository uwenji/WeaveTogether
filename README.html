TryTriWeave decides where the cells of a triaxial weave go on a shape and how many sides each one has. This practice takes that lattice (the OBJ it exports) and answers the weaver's questions: where to start, which cell to close next, which strips meet there and which one goes on top, which crossings to clip until they hold, where to cut a strand that is too long, and how to read the singular cells as a map before starting. Building a cell weaves its strips completely through each other: every crossing of the cell's fan (its ring and the opposite edge of each of its triangles) that is not made yet — so the free ends are woven, not just laid across each other (your "it intersects each lath, but it is not woven"). A cell is built in sub-steps, one strip at a time, as in your notation: first the strip that arrives from the cell before is continued through the fan (the marching strip), then the strips already in hand are crossed where they meet at this cell (lay — the strip on top is the subject), then every new strip is added in turn — threaded through the strips in hand in its weaving direction, its crossings listed as an over / under sequence — and the last sub-step closes the cell; a cell already woven by its neighbours is counted but left out of the tour; a final sub-step shows the finished weave in full colour. The History bar plays the sub-steps and, inside one, the crossings. Every strip in hand is shown whole: its woven part on the surface, the rest hanging straight off its outermost crossing.

What is computed

1 · lattice → weave js/weave.js

The lattice is the dual triangulation: a vertex per cell, a triangle per small triangular opening. Every lattice edge is a crossing (two laths meet at its midpoint), every triangle holds three short lath segments (its mid-segments), and the n segments around a vertex close an n-sided cell. A lath passes through edge midpoints and in every triangle takes the mid-segment that wraps the vertex it did not wrap in the previous triangle. The tracing, the over/under parity (over and under alternate along a strand, the two strands at a crossing differ, fixed by a parity BFS) and the three-family colouring are the ones of TryTriWeave/web/js/lattice.js (traceStrands), re-implemented here and left untouched there. On top of it: which strand and which position every segment belongs to, and the ring of segments and crossings around every cell in cyclic order. Face windings are made consistent first (one triangle of the exported Bunny was flipped) and turned outward.

The star weave's bands (js/bands.js; until the afternoon of 19 November a round was a strand of the lattice, drawn 0.70 a wide — your photograph shows the weave as it is made: three strips on every side of a hole, lying against each other; the first reading is kept as the pattern Star in rounds, below). Every strand of the lattice is a band of n strips at one pitch, centred on the strand's line; the lattice's own strand is the middle strip, the others lie beside it (S4 → S4, S4b, S4c; woven band by band the strips are named in the order they are threaded instead — S4 is then the strip nearest the centre and the lattice's own strand S4b: The star from its centre, below). Where two bands cross on a lattice edge there is a block of n × n crossings. Who is on top: away from a centre the strips of the band that is on top in the hexagonal weave — the block is woven as one crossing. At a corner of a centre (the edge runs from the centre cell to a neighbour: its two bands are two sides of the hole) the strips are counted from the hole, round 1 nearest, and the strip of the earlier round is on top; two of the same round cross as the hexagonal weave has it — the nested V. At a point of the star (the cell opposite the edge is a centre) the strip of the later round is on top: the ends of a new strip lie over the rounds before it. Between two centres every crossing of a block goes with the centre it is nearer to. Mirror image turns the hexagonal weave over and keeps "the earlier round on top". The width. A band can be as wide as closes the triangles of the hexagonal weave and no wider: its strips would meet a third band's before they are past the second's — 0.289 a on equal edges. On a lattice of unequal edges the room differs from stretch to stretch (Bunny: 0.13 … 0.28 a and more); the pattern's own width fits on every stretch — the tightest one decides (your choice on 20 November: rule A of document/star_strip_width.html, where the three rules are drawn. On the evening of the 19th the width fitted on 95 % of the stretches; on the rest — 22 of the sphere's 720, 78 of the Bunny's 1842, the short edges — three bands lay in one place and the layers could not be read). The price is thin strips where the lattice is roomier: for three strips 0.076 a instead of 0.081 on the sphere, 0.041 a instead of 0.061 on the Bunny. Display → laths → width narrows the strips or widens them up to the width that closes the triangles at the median edge (0.090 and 0.088 a; the status line then says on how many stretches the strips run into each other: 268 of 720, 864 of 1842). The thickness is held to a quarter of the width: flat strips.

Star in rounds (starRule; the star weave as it was until the afternoon of 19 November, back as a pattern of its own on the 20th — "bring the previous star weave pattern as a new pattern in our library"). The same strands as the hexagonal weave, one strip each, 0.70 a wide so that they close on each other, and another over / under: the sheet's rounds read on the lattice. The strip of round r on a side is the strand that runs between ring r − 1 and ring r of cells round the centre. At a crossing between two rings the strip of the later round is underneath (a new strip is pushed under the strips already lying across its place); at a crossing on a ring the strip of the later round is on top; two strips of the same round cross at a corner of the round with the strip of the side counter-clockwise of the corner on top, and over the middle of a side with the one from the clockwise side on top (mirror image: the other way round). Round 1 by this rule is the hexagonal weave itself. rounds says how many rounds are woven this way (0: every round, out to the rim or all the way round); the strips beyond go over and under in turn. The mad weave rule (H. A. Verrill, Symmetries of mad weave patterns, J. Math. Arts 2026: 2-1 twill both ways, the top layer is the rhombic tiling). On the √3 lattice, at a crossing on a spoke (a cell v — a new cell c) the strip that goes round v in the triangle on the left of v → c is on top; at a crossing on a short diagonal (two new cells), in a triangle (v, c1, c2) counted from its cell v, the strip that goes round c2. Checked on the flat patch, the three cones, the saddle, the sphere, the torus, the Bunny and the Y: a stack in every triangle, one strip on top of each rhombus (one rhombus per lattice edge), no run longer than three, every strip on top a third of its way.

Layers. The wide strips of the mad weave lie three deep in every triangle of the lattice: the strip that is over at both its crossings there is on top, the one that is under at both on the surface, the third between them (w.level at a crossing, w.midLevel along a segment; 0 … 2). A triangle whose three strips go round — each over the next, as in every triangle of the hexagonal weave — has no stack. The hemp leaf has three layers at a crossing (the weave's two strips at 0 and 2, the centre strip between them at 1), the star weave two.

On a fine lattice that is close enough. Now the lattice is split once (a vertex in every edge: the crossings), its control points are moved until Loop's surface passes through every crossing (to 0.01 % of a) and under every cell's vertex as deep as the ring of crossings round it asks (the lattice's own vertices stand off the weave: on the dodecahedron by 4 of 70), and it is split three times more by Loop's rules: sixteen fine edges to a lattice edge, made once per lattice in 6 – 330 ms. A strand is a straight line in the coordinates of the faces it runs through, from the middle of one edge to the middle of the next; a strip of its band is the point of the surface next to the place beside that line, with the surface's own normal there. Where it is not used. On the surface every strip of a band turns with it, and where the surface turns sharply — the rim of a capped cylinder, a tree's forks, the Bunny's ears — the flat places two strips share no longer fit side by side, while a band that is stiff across does better there (wrong rays with the surface / without: tube of 84 cells 3 / 7, 5-cone 1 / 1, capped cylinder of 104 cells 44 / 0, tree of 188 cells 266 / 19, Bunny 1530 / 22).
